## Step-by-step reasoning (persona voice)

I ran the mental walk I always do when someone asks "where does it hurt": start at the morning huddle and follow the day.

**Direction A walk.** The day starts with queues: the wire repair queue (payments that failed STP validation and will miss cut-off if nobody fixes field 57 or the beneficiary address), the ACH return queue (R-codes, 60-day unauthorized clocks), the Reg E dispute queue (10-business-day provisional credit clock ticking on every case), the suspense/nostro break report (aged items nobody wants to own), and — a few times a year — the remediation program that lands on my floor when the bank finds it's been miscalculating something for six years. Every one of these is: gather evidence from five systems, apply judgment under a deadline, produce an artifact (a repair, a letter, a GL entry, a recall message), and log it so audit doesn't kill you later. That's the shape of an agent's job. The research brief confirms the disputes/chargebacks space is quantified (261M disputes, ~$128 all-in merchant cost, $9-10 issuer processing per dispute) and that the *agent-initiated purchase* attribution problem has rails (Agent Pay tokens, Trusted Agent Protocol, Amex ACE protection) but no resolution tooling — from my seat that reads as "a new dispute reason code is coming and nobody has written the procedure for it," which is exactly the kind of thing that becomes a 400-case backlog in month two.

**Direction B walk.** The scarier list is the people. Marge on suspense breaks retires in 14 months and there is no document that says how she knows which GL to look at. The wire-room lead can look at a rejected MT103 and name the fix in four seconds; his "procedure" is 20 years of corridor-specific scar tissue. Our SOPs describe a process nobody has followed since the 2021 platform migration. New hires take 18-24 months to be safe on the exceptions desk, and we lose half of them first. Every incident bridge call reinvents knowledge we already paid for in the last incident. The teach-inward verb is genuinely unserved: vendors sell me chatbots over my stale SOPs, which is automating the wrong artifact. What I actually need is something that captures judgment *at the moment it's exercised* — case by case, on the live queue — and can then defend, replay, and transfer it.

**What changed to make this buildable now** (recurring across cards): (1) models can finally read the garbage — free-text payment fields, fax-quality remittance images, decades of case notes — that killed every prior automation attempt; (2) ISO 20022 migration means payment messages carry structured, richer data; (3) tool-use/MCP maturity means an agent can actually *act* in core systems, not just draft text; (4) long-context models can hold an entire veteran's case history or a full remediation population's rules; (5) the regulatory direction of travel (Mills Review agentic-finance framework, FCA AI Live Testing) rewards audit-trailed, HITL-gated agents — which is how ops people wanted it built anyway.

**Selection/dedup:** I dropped "generic chargeback evidence assembly" as a standalone (brief flags mature SaaS headwind) and folded evidence-assembly muscle into the Reg E caseworker where the issuer-side clock makes it distinct. I kept one big unbounded swing per direction (G1-4 remediation machine; G1-7 retirement shadow). Final: 6 Direction A + 6 Direction B.

## Direction A — Exception and dispute repair in money flows

### G1-1 · Repair Desk — the pre-cut-off payment fixer [Direction A]

1. **Name:** Repair Desk
2. **The problem:** Every day a slice of outbound wires drops out of straight-through processing into the repair queue — bad BIC, missing intermediary bank, beneficiary name stuffed into the address line, currency/country mismatch. Each one is a 5-15 minute manual fix, and they all have to be done before cut-off or the customer's payment slips a day and the phone starts ringing. On heavy days we triage by value and let the tail miss. The people who can repair fast are the same three people who can do everything else.
3. **Who has it:** Wire room / payment operations repair clerks and their supervisor at any bank running Fedwire/CHIPS/SWIFT volume; same shape exists in corporate payment factories.
4. **What the agent does:** Watches the repair queue in real time. For each fallen payment it diagnoses the STP failure, pulls the customer's payment history and standard settlement instructions, reconstructs the correct routing (right intermediary for the corridor, properly structured beneficiary fields), and *executes* the repair for low-risk cases within pre-agreed tolerances — auto-releasing before cut-off with a full before/after diff logged. Ambiguous or high-value repairs go to a human with the proposed fix, evidence, and confidence attached, sorted by time-to-cut-off. End of day it publishes a repair report: what it fixed, what it escalated, what patterns keep recurring (so the root cause upstream gets fixed too).
5. **Why newly possible now:** Repair is exactly the unstructured-text judgment task that rules engines failed at for 20 years — the information needed is usually *in* the message, just in the wrong field or in free text. LLMs read that. ISO 20022 migration adds structured fields to validate against; MCP-style connectors let the agent act in the payment platform instead of emailing a human; and audit-log/HITL patterns are now standard agent architecture, which is the only way compliance would ever sign off on auto-release.

### G1-2 · Recall Chaser — misdirected and duplicate payment recovery [Direction A]

1. **Name:** Recall Chaser
2. **The problem:** When money goes to the wrong place — fat-fingered account number, duplicate release, fraud-induced misdirection — recovery is a slow-motion chase: draft the recall request, send the indemnity, wait, chase the beneficiary bank, wait, escalate, wait. Cases sit in a spreadsheet someone updates weekly. Recovery odds decay by the hour, and nobody can tell the customer (or the regulator) the real-time status. The chase work is clerical but relentless, so it gets deprioritized, so we recover less.
3. **Who has it:** Payment investigations / funds-transfer inquiry teams; also treasury ops at corporates who send the "please claw that back" requests banks then juggle.
4. **What the agent does:** Owns the recovery case end-to-end: generates the recall/request-for-return messages in the right format for the rail (SWIFT gpi stop-and-recall, Fedwire request for return, ACH R06/R07 route), sends them, monitors gpi tracker status, and *chases on a decaying schedule* — polite nudge at 48h, escalation to the beneficiary bank's listed inquiry contact at day 5, drafts the indemnity letter when asked, flags cases for legal when the window is closing. Maintains a live recovery dashboard with per-case probability and a regulator-ready timeline of every action taken. Learns which beneficiary banks respond to what (see G1-8's precedent idea — this agent builds that corpus as a by-product).
5. **Why newly possible now:** gpi/ISO 20022 give machine-readable payment status to chase against; agents can hold and act on hundreds of long-running cases in parallel without the human limits that make chasing decay; and message drafting across rails/formats — previously the reason this needed experienced staff — is now the easy part.

### G1-3 · Reg E Caseworker — the dispute clock runner [Direction A]

1. **Name:** Reg E Caseworker
2. **The problem:** Electronic funds transfer disputes run on hard regulatory clocks — investigate and resolve in 10 business days or grant provisional credit, 45/90 days outer bounds, written determinations required. In practice each case means pulling auth logs, device/session data, transaction history, prior dispute history, maybe merchant descriptors, then writing it up. Volume is spiky, staff turns over, and the clock doesn't care. Miss deadlines and you're writing checks you shouldn't; rush determinations and you're inconsistent, which is its own regulatory problem. The brief's numbers say the pain compounds: US disputes heading to 146M/$15.3B by 2026, ~20% first-party fraud hiding inside.
3. **Who has it:** Issuer-side dispute operations analysts and their team leads at banks and card issuers; the same clock-driven shape exists for UK Payment Services Regulations disputes.
4. **What the agent does:** Runs the whole case: intakes the claim, opens the file, pulls evidence from every connected system (auth stream, 3DS results, device fingerprint, geolocation, customer contact history, prior-dispute pattern), applies the bank's adjudication standards, decides provisional credit before the clock forces it, drafts the determination and the customer letter with the evidence cited, books the GL entries, and closes with a complete audit pack. Denials and first-party-fraud suspicions always route through a human gate with the assembled case. A supervisor view shows clock risk across the whole queue.
5. **Why newly possible now:** The evidence is scattered and semi-structured — exactly what agentic retrieval + reasoning handles and what deterministic dispute software never could (those tools route and track; they don't *investigate*). Consistency-at-scale with explainable reasoning is what CFPB says it expects from AI decisions, and the audit-pack-per-case pattern is now cheap to produce.

### G1-4 · Make-Whole Machine — the remediation program in a box [Direction A]

1. **Name:** Make-Whole Machine
2. **The problem:** When the bank discovers a systemic error — fees charged wrong for six years, interest miscalculated on a product variant, disclosures not sent — a remediation program spins up: define the impacted population, compute per-account redress plus interest, generate and send letters, cut payments, handle returned mail and uncashed checks, answer the call spike, and document everything for the regulator. Today that's a 12-18 month project with an army of contractors and a program office, and it's the most soul-crushing work in operations. Every big bank runs several at once and nobody talks about it.
3. **Who has it:** Remediation/redress program managers, operations directors, and the compliance officers who own the regulator relationship. In the UK, Consumer Duty has made systematic redress capability table stakes.
4. **What the agent does:** Takes the error definition as input and runs the program: writes and executes the population-identification queries against account histories (with sampled human validation), computes redress and interest per account under the agreed methodology, generates the customer communications, initiates payments through existing rails, monitors returns/exceptions and works them (trace the new address, reissue, escheat when required), maintains the program tracker, and continuously produces the regulator evidence pack — population logic, methodology, QC sampling results, payment reconciliation. Human gates at methodology sign-off, at payment-batch release, and on every out-of-tolerance case. This is the unbounded swing: the end state is that a remediation that took 18 months and 200 contractors takes 8 weeks and a program office of five.
5. **Why newly possible now:** Remediation was never one hard problem — it's thousands of small judgment calls (does this account fit the population? what address do we trust? is this edge case in scope?) stitched to boring execution. That's the agent shape. Long-context models can hold the full methodology and its edge-case rulings and apply them consistently across a million accounts; agentic QC-sampling gives the audit story; and the alternative (contractor armies) is the single biggest discretionary ops spend at most large banks, so the ROI needs no creativity.

### G1-5 · Break Detective — aged suspense and nostro break investigator [Direction A]

1. **Name:** Break Detective
2. **The problem:** The reconciliation reports land every morning with breaks nobody wants: a nostro entry with no ledger match, a suspense-account item aging past 30/60/90 days, a one-cent-out batch that's actually two errors netting. Matching engines catch the easy 95%; the residual requires a human to *investigate* — walk the entry back across systems, read the original message, check whether ops booked something manually on a holiday cut-over. Aged breaks are audit findings waiting to happen, and the person who can clear them fastest is always the person the team can least spare.
3. **Who has it:** Reconciliation analysts and financial-control teams; every bank has a "top 20 aged items" meeting that everyone dreads.
4. **What the agent does:** Picks up the unmatched residual every night. For each break it traces lineage across ledgers, statements, payment messages, and manual-journal logs; forms a hypothesis (timing difference, duplicated booking, FX rounding, missing counter-entry, genuine loss); gathers the supporting evidence; and proposes the resolving entry — or the write-off with justification — as a ready-to-approve package. Humans approve; the agent books and documents. It also clusters breaks by root cause and tells the platform team which upstream defect is manufacturing them. Works the graveyard shift nobody staffs.
5. **Why newly possible now:** Break investigation defeated rules-based recon add-ons because every aged break is a small unique story told across unstructured artifacts (SWIFT copies, emails, journal narratives). Agents can now read all of it, keep a hypothesis tree, and act through system connectors. The 95%+ touchless benchmarks in adjacent cash application (HighRadius/Billtrust, per the brief) show what the matched-rate ceiling looks like when the residual gets automated intelligence instead of overtime.

### G1-6 · Agent-Purchase Referee — the dispute type with no procedure yet [Direction A]

1. **Name:** Agent-Purchase Referee
2. **The problem:** From where I sit, every new payment rail arrives the same way: launch first, and six months later my floor inherits a queue of disputes with no procedure, no reason codes that fit, and no evidence standards. Agent-initiated purchases are next. The networks shipped attribution rails (Mastercard Agent Pay tokens, Visa Trusted Agent Protocol) and Amex is already selling protection against agent *errors* — but when the cardholder says "I never told my agent to buy that," somebody in operations has to reconstruct what actually happened, and today there is no desk, no procedure, and no tooling for it. The brief confirms: rails exist, resolution tooling does not.
3. **Who has it:** Issuer dispute operations (first), then network dispute-resolution teams and the merchant side; ultimately the compliance officers asked "how do you adjudicate these consistently?"
4. **What the agent does:** For a contested agent-initiated transaction, reconstructs the authorization chain: pulls the agentic token/mandate, the consent scope the consumer granted, the agent's session trail, merchant-side attribution signals, and the consumer's pattern history. Adjudicates into the three buckets that matter — consumer authorized (dispute fails), agent erred within a valid mandate (route to agent-error protection / merchant), or fraud/token misuse (route to fraud liability) — and produces the evidence-cited determination plus the correctly formatted network filing. Builds the precedent base and the *procedure manual* for this dispute class as it works, so the bank has an examinable standard before regulators ask for one.
5. **Why newly possible now:** The dispute class literally did not exist 15 months ago; the attribution data (tokens, agent identity protocols) only now exists to reason over; and no incumbent dispute vendor has shipped anything here. First mover gets to define the evidence standard — in disputes, whoever writes the procedure everyone else copies owns the category.

## Direction B — Expertise capture and transfer inside the institution

### G1-7 · Last Twelve Months — the retirement shadow [Direction B]

1. **Name:** Last Twelve Months
2. **The problem:** Marge retires in 14 months. She's cleared suspense breaks for 23 years, and what she knows — which GL to check first, which branch always fat-fingers holiday postings, which counterparty's statements lie — exists nowhere but her head. The "knowledge transfer plan" HR will run is two weeks of shadowing and a Word document. We lose someone like her every quarter now; the demographic math is brutal, and every exit resets a piece of the desk to junior speed.
3. **Who has it:** Operations managers with retiring specialists in scarce seats — recon veterans, wire-room leads, senior adjudicators, workout support staff; HR/L&D owns the "knowledge risk" line on the risk register but has no tool.
4. **What the agent does:** Attaches to the expert's live queue for their final year. For every case the expert closes, it records the full context and disposition; when the disposition is non-obvious (deviates from what the agent would have predicted), it asks *one short question* at a moment the expert chooses — "why the write-off here and not a re-trace?" — and encodes the answer against the case. Over months it builds a validated decision playbook: rules with the exceptions attached to real precedents, tested continuously by predicting the expert's next dispositions on held-out cases and measuring agreement. At retirement, the bank keeps a queryable, evidence-cited "what would Marge do" oracle plus a structured curriculum for the successor — and a scored gap list of the judgments it never got confident on, so the last months of overlap target exactly those.
5. **Why newly possible now:** Capture-at-the-moment-of-judgment was always the right method and always operationally impossible — no human apprentice can shadow every case, and interviews retrieve what experts *think* they do, not what they do. An agent can watch 100% of the queue, notice only the surprising 5%, and keep the interruption cost near zero. Long-context models hold years of case history; the validation loop (predict-then-compare) turns "knowledge capture" from a documentation exercise into a measured artifact with an accuracy number — which is what makes it real.

### G1-8 · Ask Marge — the precedent oracle for the exceptions desk [Direction B]

1. **Name:** Ask Marge
2. **The problem:** The most valuable sentence on my floor is "we've seen this before." A weird break, a strange return code combination, a counterparty behaving oddly — the veteran remembers the 2019 case that looked the same and what worked. The junior doesn't, so they either escalate (slow), guess (dangerous), or ask the veteran (who is a bottleneck and, see G1-7, leaving). Our case-management systems technically contain all this history; nobody can find anything in them because the knowledge is in free-text resolution notes written at 6pm.
3. **Who has it:** Every exceptions-desk analyst under 5 years' tenure; team leads who spend half their day being the human search engine; QA teams trying to enforce consistency without a precedent base.
4. **What the agent does:** Ingests the desk's full resolved-case corpus — notes, attachments, dispositions, outcomes — and turns it into a live precedent system. When a new case lands, it *acts before being asked*: attaches the three most similar prior cases with what was done, what worked, who approved it, and where this case differs; drafts the disposition the precedents support; and flags when the queue is drifting from precedent (same fact pattern, different outcomes across analysts — a consistency problem QA would want to know about). Analysts confirm or override; overrides become new precedent with the reasoning captured, so the corpus compounds instead of rotting.
5. **Why newly possible now:** The corpus already exists in every case-management system — it was just unreadable at scale until LLMs could parse ten years of shorthand resolution notes. Semantic similarity over fact patterns (not keywords) is what makes precedent retrieval actually match how veterans think. And the compounding loop — every disposition enriches the base — only works if capture is zero-effort, which agent-side drafting finally makes true.

### G1-9 · Drift Auditor — the SOP-versus-reality agent [Direction B]

1. **Name:** Drift Auditor
2. **The problem:** Our procedures describe a bank that stopped existing two platform migrations ago. Actual practice lives in workarounds: the extra check Priya does because the system misses a case, the "everyone knows you skip step 4" shortcut, the manual log a team keeps because the official report is wrong. Some workarounds are the real control; some are control *breaks* nobody has risk-assessed. When the audit comes, we discover the gap the hard way; when a veteran leaves, the undocumented workaround leaves with them and something silently stops being checked.
3. **Who has it:** Operations team leads (own the SOPs, no time to update them), operational-risk and internal-audit teams (own the gap when it surfaces), and every new joiner trained on documents that don't match the desk.
4. **What the agent does:** Reads the documented procedure, then observes the actual work — case-system event logs, approval trails, the sequence and timing of what people really do. It diffs practice against procedure and classifies each divergence: undocumented improvement (draft the SOP update and route for sign-off), risky shortcut (flag to the team lead with the specific control it bypasses), or dead procedure step (propose retirement). It maintains the SOP as a living document — every proposed change traced to observed evidence — and produces a quarterly "procedure health" report ops risk can take to the audit committee. The workaround knowledge that used to walk out the door gets promoted into the official record while its author is still here to confirm it.
5. **Why newly possible now:** Process-mining tools could see event sequences but couldn't read the SOP or explain the divergence; agents do both sides — read the prose procedure, read the behavioral logs, and reason about whether the gap is wisdom or risk. Drafting the corrected SOP (the step that never got done because it's tedious) is now free, which flips documentation from a periodic project to a continuous by-product of work.

### G1-10 · Nightmare Simulator — the exceptions-desk flight simulator [Direction B]

1. **Name:** Nightmare Simulator
2. **The problem:** It takes 18-24 months to grow a safe exceptions analyst, because competence comes from having *seen* the rare ugly cases — and you can't schedule when a cross-border duplicate with a bankrupt intermediary shows up. So juniors learn on live customer money, seniors spend their scarce time supervising, and half the cohort quits before they're useful. Classroom training teaches the happy path; the job is the unhappy path.
3. **Who has it:** Operations training teams and team leads carrying the supervision load; ultimately the ops director whose error rates spike every time tenure dips.
4. **What the agent does:** Mines the desk's historical case corpus for the genuinely hard patterns, then *generates* synthetic training cases with seeded ground truth — realistic messages, statements, customer contacts, red herrings included — and runs juniors through them in a sandboxed replica of the case workflow. It plays every counterparty (the vague customer, the slow beneficiary bank, the pushy branch), grades the trainee's investigation and disposition against the veteran playbook (fed by G1-7/G1-8), coaches on the specific reasoning gap, and certifies readiness per case-type with evidence. New failure modes seen on the live desk become next week's scenarios automatically.
5. **Why newly possible now:** Generating coherent multi-artifact case scenarios (messages + histories + a consistent hidden truth) was impossible before generative models; so was playing a realistic counterparty in the loop. Seeded ground truth makes grading objective rather than opinion-based. Flight simulators transformed pilot training exactly this way — ops apprenticeship is the same problem and has never had one.

### G1-11 · Incident Scribe — the living runbook for the bad days [Direction B]

1. **Name:** Incident Scribe
2. **The problem:** The worst knowledge loss isn't daily-queue knowledge — it's bad-day knowledge. Batch fails at 4:50pm before a long weekend; Fedwire extends; the file from the card processor comes in double. The people who know what to do learned it in previous incidents, and what they know lives in memory and a 300-message bridge-call chat that nobody ever reads again. Every incident we pay full price to relearn what the last incident taught, and the two people who remember the 2022 cut-over fiasco are both eligible for the early-retirement package.
3. **Who has it:** Ops incident managers, shift supervisors, and business-continuity owners; the payments ops director who personally gets the 4:50pm call because nobody else knows the drill.
4. **What the agent does:** Two modes. *After* every incident, it ingests the bridge chat, timelines, tickets, and a short debrief it conducts with participants ("what did you check first, and why?"), and folds the lessons into a living, versioned runbook — decision points, thresholds, who-to-call, what worked, what wasted an hour. *During* the next incident, it's first responder: recognizes the pattern ("this matches the 2024-11 duplicate-file event, 83% similar"), surfaces the exact relevant runbook section and prior-incident timeline, drafts the comms and the recovery checklist, and records the new incident's deviations for the next debrief. The runbook compounds; the retirement package stops being a continuity risk.
5. **Why newly possible now:** The raw material (chat transcripts, tickets, timelines) is already captured but was never synthesizable — no human writes the post-mortem properly at 11pm after the fix. Agents do the synthesis for free and, more importantly, can *retrieve under pressure*: matching a live incident to precedent in seconds is exactly the veteran's remembered-pattern skill, externalized.

### G1-12 · Handoff Guardian — capturing the supervisor's triage brain [Direction B]

1. **Name:** Handoff Guardian
2. **The problem:** Follow-the-sun ops runs on handoffs, and handoffs run on whatever the outgoing shift had energy to type. The judgment in a good handoff — *these* three cases will breach clock by 11am, *that* counterparty needs chasing before Frankfurt closes, don't release the batch until the recon clears — is supervisor triage expertise, and it's applied fresh at every shift boundary or not at all. Bad handoffs are where deadlines get missed and where the same case gets worked twice or zero times. The supervisors who write great handoffs can't be cloned; their prioritization logic is pure tacit knowledge.
3. **Who has it:** Shift supervisors and analysts in any follow-the-sun or multi-site operation — payments, recon, disputes, settlements; the ops director who sees breach clusters at shift boundaries and can't fix them with a template.
4. **What the agent does:** Reads the full day's case activity across the queue — clocks, aging, pending external responses, cut-off calendar — and *writes the handoff the best supervisor would write*: ranked risks with reasons, deadline math made explicit, recommended first hour for the incoming shift. It learns the ranking by watching which items veteran supervisors actually flag and how incoming shifts sequence work, and it improves against a measurable target: clock breaches and double-handling at shift boundaries. It also briefs interactively — the incoming lead interrogates it ("why is this above that?") and gets the evidence. Over time it becomes the codified triage standard across all sites, not just the shifts that happen to have the good supervisor.
5. **Why newly possible now:** Triage judgment over a whole queue needs full-queue context plus deadline arithmetic plus knowledge of counterparty behavior — too much for a template, trivial for a long-context agent connected to the case system. The learning signal (what veterans flag; what breaches anyway) is already in the data. And unlike a dashboards project, the artifact is the *narrative with reasons* — which is the part humans actually read at 6am.
