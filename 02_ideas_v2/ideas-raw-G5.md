## Direction A — Machine-speed negotiation between parties

### G5-1 — RateWatch: continuous processor-fee renegotiation (Direction A)

1. **Name:** RateWatch
2. **The problem:** Every merchant I've ever worked with signs a payment-processing deal, gets a good rate for six months, and then the effective take silently creeps — downgrades, new network fees passed through at a markup, tiers that stop matching their mix. Nobody re-opens the contract because auditing a merchant statement is miserable and the renegotiation takes a quarter of someone's life. The processor is betting on your inattention, and the bet pays.
3. **Who has it:** CFO / head of payments at mid-market merchants and platforms ($10M–$1B volume); secondarily the payments PM at a marketplace who owns unit economics.
4. **What the agent does:** Ingests monthly processor statements and interchange tables, reconstructs the true effective cost versus the contracted schedule, detects drift and misclassified downgrades, builds a leverage dossier (benchmark rates for the merchant's exact MCC/mix, competing processor quotes it solicits itself), then opens and runs the renegotiation with the processor's account team — drafting the redline, escalating through email/portal, and if the processor stonewalls, executing a routing shift or an RFP to alternatives. Runs continuously, not annually.
5. **Why newly possible now:** Statement parsing at interchange-line granularity finally works (multimodal models read the ugly PDFs); agent-to-agent email negotiation is credible now that reasoning models can hold a multi-round bargaining position within bounds; and outcome pricing ("% of dollars recovered") is now a normalized way to buy this — a16z/Bessemer's whole 2025-26 thesis. Wedge economics: free audit, take-rate on savings, zero integration to start.

### G5-2 — YieldFloor: the treasurer's deposit-pricing negotiator (Direction A)

1. **Name:** YieldFloor
2. **The problem:** Corporate treasurers leave enormous yield on the table because deposit pricing is a relationship negotiation, not a market. Your ECR and money-market rates were set when the account opened; the bank reprices down fast and up never. Renegotiating means a lunch and three weeks of email with your relationship manager, so it happens once a year if ever. Multiply the inattention across every operating account and it's real basis points on real balances.
3. **Who has it:** Corporate treasurer / assistant treasurer at mid-market companies and funds; the person who knows they're under-earning but can't justify the time to fight about 40bps per bank.
4. **What the agent does:** Monitors balances, current rate sheets, and the live rate environment; computes what each balance *should* earn given size and stickiness; opens rate negotiations with each bank's RM on a cadence the human never could (monthly, per-account); solicits competing bids from other banks as credible leverage; drafts and sends the "match this or we move the operating balance" letter; and on approval executes the sweep or the move. Every negotiation is logged with the counterfactual, so the take-rate on recaptured yield is provable.
5. **Why newly possible now:** Bank rate data and account APIs (open-banking-grade connectivity) make the counterfactual computable; reasoning models can run a bounded, mandate-limited negotiation without going rogue; and the bank side is simultaneously deploying agents (nearly half of Tier-1 banks by 2026), which means the counterparty will increasingly answer at machine speed — the first two-sided negotiation loop in treasury. Distribution wedge: sell to treasurers as pure upside on a contingency fee.

### G5-3 — Accord: the always-open settlement desk for delinquent debt (Direction A)

1. **Name:** Accord
2. **The problem:** Salient and Kastle proved AI can *call* delinquent borrowers, and payment rates go up 20-30%. But the actual negotiation — what plan, what settlement percentage, what forbearance — is still a static decision matrix a human approved last year. Real bargaining is bilateral and situational: the borrower's capacity changes weekly, and the lender's optimal offer changes with roll-rate forecasts. Both sides lose because neither can afford to renegotiate continuously; accounts roll to charge-off that a better-timed, better-shaped offer would have cured.
3. **Who has it:** Head of collections / loss mitigation at a card issuer or installment lender; the portfolio manager who watches cure rates and charge-offs; on the other side, the borrower who would settle if the offer met them where they actually are.
4. **What the agent does:** For each delinquent account, runs a genuine negotiation loop: models the borrower's payment capacity from behavior, generates a policy-bounded offer space (plan lengths, settlement floors, fee waivers approved by credit policy), negotiates iteratively — over text, email, voice, or against the borrower's own money-management agent — and *re-opens* the negotiation automatically when circumstances shift (missed promise, new deposit pattern, hardship signal, at which point it escalates to a human per Reg-F-style guardrails). Executes the agreed plan into the servicing system and produces the full audit artifact per account.
5. **Why newly possible now:** Voice/text agents in collections are proven infrastructure (Salient at ~$25M ARR); what's new is that reasoning models can hold a multi-round bargaining position inside hard policy rails with a complete audit trail — the exact "ironclad human-in-the-loop gates" Series-A investors now demand — and consumer-side money agents are appearing, so machine-to-machine settlement negotiation has a counterparty for the first time.

### G5-4 — MarginMatch: machine-speed collateral and margin-call negotiation (Direction A)

1. **Name:** MarginMatch
2. **The problem:** Margin calls and collateral disputes between counterparties still run on email and spreadsheets. Two ops teams disagree on a mark, the CSA says one thing, the portfolio says another, and the dispute sits for days while exposure sits unhedged. The negotiation over *what collateral, at what haircut, delivered when* is mechanical — both sides' positions are computable from documents they already signed — but it crawls at human speed and every day of delay is real counterparty risk.
3. **Who has it:** Collateral operations lead / margin ops at a bank, asset manager, or hedge fund; the treasurer who eats the funding cost of over-collateralization posted "to be safe" because disputing was too slow.
4. **What the agent does:** Reads the governing agreements (CSA terms, eligible collateral schedules, thresholds, MTAs), independently reprices the disputed exposure from market data, identifies exactly where the two calculations diverge, and negotiates the resolution with the counterparty's ops team — or their agent — at machine speed: proposes the reconciling adjustment, drafts the dispute notice or the agreement, selects optimal eligible collateral (cheapest-to-deliver within the schedule), and executes the movement instruction on human approval. Continuously renegotiates collateral optimization, not just at dispute time.
5. **Why newly possible now:** Contract-reasoning quality is proven at Harvey/Legora level, so CSAs are machine-readable in practice; both sides of the street are deploying ops agents simultaneously, so the counterparty can finally answer in minutes; and eval/observability tooling makes a "show your work" reconciliation artifact standard, which is what makes ops leaders trust an agent to send a dispute notice.

### G5-5 — TermFlow: the live market for early-payment terms (Direction A)

1. **Name:** TermFlow
2. **The problem:** "2/10 net 30" is a negotiation frozen in a template decades ago. The right discount for paying early is a live function of both sides' cash positions and rates — it changes daily — but nobody renegotiates payment terms invoice-by-invoice because the negotiation cost exceeds the spread. So buyers sit on cash they'd happily deploy, suppliers take 30%+ APR factoring instead of offering 12% to their own customer, and the spread goes to intermediaries. Fazeshift and Daylit automated the *chasing* of invoices; nobody automated the *bargaining* over their terms.
3. **Who has it:** Supplier-side CFO/controller who needs cash-flow smoothing without factoring costs; buyer-side AP/treasury lead with idle cash earning less than the discount on offer.
4. **What the agent does:** Sits on top of existing AR/AP systems on both sides. The supplier-side agent knows the true urgency of cash (payroll dates, covenant headroom); the buyer-side agent knows the true value of holding it. Per invoice — or per relationship — the agents negotiate the early-payment discount as a live two-sided price, close the bargain within each side's mandate, and execute: payment released, discount memo posted, both ERPs reconciled. Over time it renegotiates *standing* terms when the negotiated history shows the template is stale.
5. **Why newly possible now:** The AR-agent layer just got funded and deployed (Fazeshift, Daylit), so the endpoints exist; agent payment rails (x402-style, AWS AgentCore Payments) make small, frequent, agent-initiated settlements cheap enough that per-invoice bargaining clears economically; and dual-sided agent presence turns what was dynamic-discounting *software* (one side sets a curve, take it or leave it) into actual negotiation.

### G5-6 — The Bourse: a bounded negotiation rail for institutional agents (Direction A — big swing)

1. **Name:** The Bourse
2. **The problem:** Every idea above is point-to-point, and here's what I learned shipping payments: point-to-point winners eventually get rebuilt on a rail. When both sides of a fee negotiation, a syndication allocation, or a vendor renewal have agents, the hard problem isn't the bargaining — it's the *envelope*: proving your counterparty's agent has authority to bind, that its mandate covers this deal size, that the transcript is admissible when someone disputes the outcome, and that neither agent can be prompt-injected into giving away the book. No envelope, no delegation; no delegation, and all of this stays email.
3. **Who has it:** Any institution whose counterparties are deploying negotiation agents — syndication desks, bank vendor-management offices, corporate treasury, collateral ops — and specifically the GC/risk officer who currently vetoes agent-to-agent dealing because there's no accountability structure.
4. **What the agent does:** It's a rail plus a resident referee agent. Institutions register negotiation agents with verified identity and signed mandates (deal-size caps, walk-away floors, escalation triggers — the mandate itself machine-readable and countersigned by a human officer). Counterparty agents then negotiate *inside* the rail: the referee agent validates authority before the first offer, enforces mandate bounds mid-negotiation, notarizes every round into a tamper-evident transcript, halts on manipulation patterns (one agent probing another's floor via injection), and issues the binding term sheet both institutions' systems can execute on. Fee and rate renewals that took a quarter close in an afternoon, continuously.
5. **Why newly possible now:** The pieces just got funded separately and nobody has composed them: agent identity/KYA (Skyfire, Oasis at $120M), agent payments (x402 with Visa/Google/AWS/Anthropic as members), and audit/eval infrastructure (Braintrust-class). The x402 Foundation proved consortium rails for agent *payments* can get major-logo adoption in a year; negotiation is the obvious next verb. Whoever owns the envelope takes basis points on everything negotiated through it — the classic network take-rate — and it's unclaimed.

## Direction B — Insuring and warranting agentic action

### G5-7 — Bindable: the underwriting desk for agent operators (Direction B)

1. **Name:** Bindable
2. **The problem:** Every enterprise deal I've watched for an action-taking agent stalls at the same clause: indemnification. The vendor is a 20-person startup that can't stand behind a $5M error; the buyer's risk officer won't sign without someone standing behind it; the incumbent insurer has no idea how to price "our AI pays invoices." AIUC raised on certifying agents, but certification isn't capacity — someone still has to *underwrite* the operator and put a limit on paper. Until that desk exists, autonomy adoption is rationed by legal, not by capability.
3. **Who has it:** Founders/CROs of agent vendors trying to close enterprise deals (they'd pay today); enterprise procurement and risk officers who need a bonded counterparty; MGAs and insurers who want the premium but lack the technical underwriting.
4. **What the agent does:** An underwriting agent that does the technical diligence no human actuary can: ingests the applicant's eval results, guardrail configuration, action logs, HITL gate design, model/version history, and incident record; runs its own adversarial probes against the applicant's agent in a sandbox; produces a quantified risk profile (expected loss per 10K actions by action class); prices and issues the policy or performance bond as an MGA product with real carrier capacity behind it; and re-underwrites continuously — every model swap or scope expansion triggers automated re-rating, not an annual form.
5. **Why newly possible now:** Agents now generate exactly the telemetry underwriting needs (standardized evals, observability traces, action logs) — this data didn't exist in auditable form before 2025. Insurance capital is actively hunting AI-native programs (insurtech AI took 95.2% of Q1-2026 funding), and AIUC's cert framework creates the *baseline standard* an underwriter can price against, the way building codes preceded fire insurance at scale.

### G5-8 — PerAct: the per-action warranty rail (Direction B)

1. **Name:** PerAct
2. **The problem:** Annual liability policies are the wrong shape for agent risk. An agent's risk isn't smooth across a year — it's lumpy per action: this $200K wire is riskier than those 10,000 routine reconciliations. Buying one blanket limit means overpaying for the routine and being underinsured for the spike. What both sides actually want is what payments already has: a tiny, priced guarantee attached to *each transaction*, like interchange carries fraud liability shifting.
3. **Who has it:** Platforms whose agents execute financial actions (payments agents, AP/AR agents, trading-adjacent ops agents) and their enterprise customers; the CFO who wants agent losses to be a predictable cost-of-goods line, not a tail-risk conversation.
4. **What the agent does:** A pricing-and-binding agent that sits in the action path: for each proposed agent action, it scores risk in real time (action type, amount, counterparty novelty, the acting agent's recent eval drift, prompt-injection indicators), quotes a warranty premium in basis points, binds cover the instant the action executes, and settles the premium over agent-payment rails. On failure, a claims agent auto-adjudicates from the action trace and pays out without a human claims process for small losses. High-risk actions get quoted a prohibitive rate — which is itself the control: pricing becomes the real-time governor on agent behavior.
5. **Why newly possible now:** x402-class rails (>100M payments) make micro-premium collection economically viable for the first time; per-action risk scoring is feasible because every modern agent action carries a structured trace; and the interchange analogy gives buyers an existing mental model — bps-per-transaction with liability attached is how finance already prices trust. Wedge: embed in one funded agent-payments platform, then it's infrastructure.

### G5-9 — Recourse: the forensic claims adjuster for agent incidents (Direction B)

1. **Name:** Recourse
2. **The problem:** When an agent misroutes funds or breaches a mandate, the loss is instant but the fault question is a swamp: was it the operator's bad mandate, the model vendor's regression, the tool provider's broken API, or third-party prompt injection (which is a *crime*, not negligence — a different policy trigger entirely)? Today that gets settled by whoever has better lawyers, which means insurers price the whole category with a fat ambiguity load, and buyers can't get subrogation. No credible fault-finding, no functioning insurance market.
3. **Who has it:** Insurers/MGAs writing agent liability (they need adjusters who can read traces); enterprises pursuing recovery from vendors after an incident; agent vendors defending against blame for what was actually a client-side mandate error.
4. **What the agent does:** A forensic agent that reconstructs the incident end-to-end from observability traces, tool-call logs, model/version records, and mandate documents: builds the causal chain, classifies the proximate cause against policy language (operator error / vendor regression / tool failure / adversarial attack), quantifies the loss allocation across parties, drafts the adjudication report to an evidentiary standard, and files the subrogation demand or claim decision. Both sides can retain it; its precedent database compounds into the case law of agent liability.
5. **Why newly possible now:** The observability layer just became standard equipment (Braintrust at $800M, Langfuse into ClickHouse) — for the first time, agent incidents leave a complete machine-readable record, which is what adjusters have never had for software failures. And the incident volume is arriving on schedule: 67% of security leaders already suspect agents accessed out-of-scope data. Someone becomes the trusted referee; being first is the moat, because precedent compounds.

### G5-10 — Telematic: usage-based insurance for agent fleets (Direction B)

1. **Name:** Telematic
2. **The problem:** Flat-rated agent insurance punishes good operators and subsidizes cowboys — same premium whether your agent has ironclad HITL gates and clean evals or was duct-taped together last sprint. Auto insurance solved this with the telematics dongle: drive well, pay less. Nobody has built the dongle for agents, so underwriters can't see behavior and safe operators can't monetize their discipline. Deployment decisions inside enterprises stall because the risk function can't distinguish a safe agent from a reckless one.
3. **Who has it:** Enterprises running growing agent fleets (the CIO/CRO pair negotiating coverage); insurers who want to write agent risk but can't differentiate it; agent vendors who'd use a verified safety score as a sales weapon.
4. **What the agent does:** A monitoring agent that rides alongside the insured fleet: continuously scores behavior against the policy's safety conditions (mandate adherence, escalation compliance, eval-drift, out-of-scope access attempts, guardrail hit rates), issues signed attestations that reprice the premium monthly, catches risk regressions *before* they become claims (a model update that degrades refusal behavior triggers an alert and a temporary rate adjustment — or an automatic scope-down of the agent's permissions, the actuarial version of a speed limiter), and feeds anonymized loss data into a cross-operator actuarial pool — the Verisk-style loss bureau this category completely lacks.
5. **Why newly possible now:** The sensor layer exists off-the-shelf (evals, observability, non-human IAM at $120M+ funding) but nobody has composed it into an *actuarial* instrument; continuous re-rating needs machine-speed attestation, which only became possible when the attestor itself became an agent. The two-sided moat is the loss-data pool: whoever aggregates cross-operator incident data first owns pricing accuracy forever — that's the Verisk playbook, and in this category the seat is empty.

### G5-11 — ClawNet: the recovery-and-loss network for agent payments (Direction B — big swing)

1. **Name:** ClawNet
2. **The problem:** Here's the thing the x402 crowd doesn't put in the press release: >100M agent payments and effectively *no chargeback rail*. Card networks won consumers because Reg E/Z and dispute rights made it safe to transact with strangers; agent payments today are wire-transfer-grade — final, unprotected, no recourse when your agent pays the wrong party, pays twice, or gets socially engineered by a counterparty agent. Every treasury team that looks at agentic payments sees this and caps the pilot at pocket change. The missing consumer-protection layer is the actual adoption bottleneck.
3. **Who has it:** Enterprises whose agents transact (treasury caps their exposure today); agent-payment platforms and the x402 ecosystem, whose volume growth is throttled by the trust ceiling; ultimately any merchant or service selling to agent buyers who wants "protected" status.
4. **What the agent does:** A network, not a policy. Members' agent payments carry ClawNet protection for a bps fee. When a payment goes wrong, a recovery agent activates within seconds — not days: traces the funds across the rail, fires recall/freeze requests at receiving institutions at machine speed, negotiates clawback directly with the counterparty's agent, and adjudicates fault from both parties' transaction traces under network rules (a private Reg E for agents: error resolution timelines, liability tiers keyed to whether each side ran verified-mandate agents). Unrecovered, in-scope losses are paid from the mutualized loss pool. Verified-safe operators get lower loss-tier liability — importing the card networks' liability-shift mechanic to push the whole ecosystem toward safer agents.
5. **Why newly possible now:** The payment rail just reached real volume (x402: >100M payments since May 2025, Visa/Google/AWS/Anthropic in the foundation; Fireblocks and AWS shipping agentic-payment suites in May 2026) but shipped without a dispute layer — the exact gap card networks filled to win the last payments era. Machine-speed recovery is *only* possible with agents (recall requests in seconds beat fund dissipation), so the incumbent manual-chargeback playbook can't be ported over — a rare case where the startup's product is structurally impossible for the incumbent process to replicate. Take-rate on every protected payment; network effects on the loss pool and rulebook.

### G5-12 — Backstop: outcome warranties for agents-as-labor vendors (Direction B)

1. **Name:** Backstop
2. **The problem:** The whole 2025-26 thesis is outcome pricing — per ticket resolved, per dollar collected. But there's an unpriced asymmetry inside it: the vendor gets paid when the agent succeeds, and the *customer* eats it when the agent succeeds *wrongly* — the "resolved" ticket that misquoted a refund policy, the "collected" dollar that came with an FDCPA-violating call. Enterprises know this, which is why outcome-priced deals still crawl through six months of indemnification redlining. Vendors can't warrant their outcomes because they can't absorb the tail; buyers won't buy unwarranted outcomes at scale.
3. **Who has it:** Outcome-priced agent vendors (the Sierra/Salient/Decagon generation and every Basis-style vertical behind them) trying to shorten enterprise sales cycles; buy-side procurement and risk teams who need a warranted counterparty; the vendor's own CFO, who needs the tail off the balance sheet before the next raise.
4. **What the agent does:** Structures, prices, and administers outcome warranties as an embedded product: analyzes the vendor's eval corpus and production traces to price "cost per defective outcome" by workflow; issues the warranty that rides each outcome-priced contract ("every resolved ticket is warranted to $X against defined defect classes"); monitors production continuously so warranty pricing tracks the vendor's actual quality drift; and when a defect claim comes in, adjudicates it from the outcome trace and pays fast — the warranty artifact drops into the vendor's security-review packet next to their SOC 2. Distribution is through vendors (they pay, since it compresses their sales cycle), but the protection runs to buyers.
5. **Why newly possible now:** Outcome pricing just became the *norm* (Crescendo literally prices per resolution at ~$1.25), which creates a countable, warrantable unit that per-seat software never had; eval infrastructure gives the warranty writer the actuarial base; and the competitive moment is sharp — the first vertical-agent vendor to sell *warranted outcomes* forces the whole category to follow, and Backstop is positioned as the one selling the warranties to everyone, the classic picks-and-shovels take.
