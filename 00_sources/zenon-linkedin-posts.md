### Post 2 — Zenon · 1w ago [~2026-07-07]

Most AI projects don't fail at the technology. They fail at the handoff.

The model works. The pilot results look promising. Leadership signs off.
And then — three months into rollout — nobody's using it.

Three places where it breaks down:

🔷 **Change management treated as a communication plan.**
Sending an email announcing a new tool isn't change management. The people whose workflows are being disrupted need to understand why the change is happening, what they're expected to do differently, and what happens if the outputs are wrong. Without that, the path of least resistance is always the old process.

🔷 **Operational impact scoped too late.**
AI doesn't just automate tasks — it shifts who owns decisions and where errors surface. When that's not mapped before deployment, you end up with teams that don't know what to escalate, managers who don't know what to review, and a system that technically runs but practically doesn't.

🔷 **No one is accountable for adoption.**
Pilots have champions. Full rollouts often don't. When there's no one whose job it is to close the gap between "deployed" and "used," that gap stays open. The tool becomes shelfware. The business case evaporates.

The companies that get past this treat adoption as a deliverable — not an outcome that happens automatically once the technology is in place.

If your AI rollout has a technical workstream and a change workstream, but only one of them has a project manager, you already know which one is behind.

### Post 10 — Zenon · 2mo ago [~2026-05-15]

Most companies treat AI deployment as the finish line. It's actually day one.

The first model you deploy is never the best version. It's the baseline. It was trained on historical data, built around assumptions you had at the time, and optimized for problems you understood before you went live.

Then reality hits. Edge cases the model never saw. Payer rules that changed. Customer behavior that shifted. Institutional knowledge sitting in the heads of your best people that never made it into the training data.

Companies that plateau are the ones that deployed and moved on. Companies that compound are the ones that built feedback loops in from the start — capturing what the model got right, what it missed, why a human overrode it, and what that override actually means.

Take Dragon Copilot. Every patient encounter is a training signal. The first version is a generic model. Six months in — if you built feedback loops correctly — it's a model trained on your doctors, your workflows, your exceptions. That's not a deployment. That's a compounding asset.

That's how institutional knowledge stops walking out the door every time someone leaves. It gets encoded. The system gets smarter with every decision, every exception, every correction.

For PE-backed companies, this is the difference between a one-time efficiency gain and a compounding asset. A model that improves 2% a month doesn't look like much at month one. At month eighteen, it's a moat.

The question to ask before any AI deployment isn't just "does this work?" It's "have we built the infrastructure to make it better?"

The first version is never the best version. Build for what it becomes.

🔷 **What many PE-backed companies are doing**

A lot of PE-backed companies are treating AI like a software rollout.

Buy licenses. Hand them out. Hope productivity shows up in EBITDA.

That is fine for experimentation.

It is not a transformation strategy.

🔷 **Why that falls short**

Giving a portfolio company LLM licenses can absolutely help individuals move faster. Better emails. Faster notes. Quicker analysis.

But that is very different from redesigning the work itself.

And that is where real value gets created.

🔷 **What this looks like in commercial functions**

A sales team with AI licenses may write better outreach.

A sales team with an AI-enabled operating model changes lead prioritization, account coverage, pricing decisions, and next-best actions across the funnel.

Same tool category. Very different outcome.

🔷 **What this looks like in operations**

The same pattern shows up in operations.

Giving agents a copilot to summarize calls may save a few minutes.

Redesigning the workflow so AI handles intake, triage, case preparation, and escalation changes labor allocation and throughput.

That is where EBITDA starts to move.

🔷 **Why this is easy to misread**

FTI's 2026 Private Equity AI Radar found that 95% of funds said their AI initiatives met or exceeded the original business case. But many of those business cases were conservatively scoped, and talent remains the top constraint to scaling.

🔷 **What actually separates the winners**

BCG's broader point is the important one: AI creates durable advantage when companies change how work gets done, not when they simply layer tools on top of current processes.

The firms that pull ahead will not be the ones with the most licenses.

They will be the ones that use AI to redesign operating models inside the portfolio.

### Post 19 — Zenon · 2mo ago [~2026-05-15]

Collections teams waste too much time on blind dialing.

Wrong borrower. Wrong time. Wrong script. Then your best agents spend the first half of every call just figuring out what is going on.

There is a better way to route that work.

Instead of treating collections as a generic call-center workflow, build a system that routes cases more intelligently from the first touch:

🔷 an ML model to separate can't pay from won't pay
🔷 outreach timed to the borrower's likely availability
🔷 Voice AI to gather facts before a human gets involved
🔷 agent handoff with a case brief and recommended next action

The goal is not to replace collectors. It is to stop using them as expensive dialers and start using them where judgment actually matters.

### Post 20 — Zenon · 3mo ago [~2026-04-15]

Most mid-size companies do not have an AI problem. They have a data foundation problem.

We see it all the time: the use case is real, the team is excited, and the pilot gets moving fast.

Then the work stalls because the data is scattered across systems, trapped in spreadsheets, or dependent on the one person who knows how everything actually fits together.

That is usually the real answer to the question, "How long will this AI project take?"

If the data layer is messy, it takes longer. If it is clean, connected, and usable, most companies can get that foundation in place in about 4–8 weeks. Not a transformation program. Not a two-year roadmap. Just the work required to make the next AI initiative actually hold.

BCG recently put a number on this: companies that build AI on mature digital infrastructure reach time to value 40% faster. In PE, the firms doing this systematically are seeing nearly 2x ROIC.

AI is not usually slowed down by the model. It is slowed down by the mess underneath it.
Fix that first.
