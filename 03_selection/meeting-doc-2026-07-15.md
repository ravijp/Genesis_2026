## The rules of the game (what we're actually judged on)

- Judges score five things: **client impact 25% · technical depth 25% · can-it-really-be-built 25% · originality 15% · presentation 10%.** There is also an **AI judge that reads our code repository** — clean code, tests, and a working setup guide literally score points.
- We get roughly **6 weeks of build time** on top of our day jobs, with a team of 3.
- **No real client data allowed** — we must create our own realistic test data, and doing that well is itself scored.
- We must ship: a working prototype, a 1–2 page brief, three one-pagers (client impact / accuracy-cost-speed / path to production), and a 1-minute video.

To keep this simple, every idea below is judged against the same **five plain questions**:

1. **Would a client pay for this?**
2. **Is there real engineering in it?**
3. **Can 3 people build and demo it in 6 weeks?**
4. **Has anyone already built this?**
5. **Does it demo well in 5 minutes?**

**One warning before the table.** Our idea pipeline ranked ideas by *which client would sign fastest*. The competition pays for something different: a genuinely new, well-engineered, demoable prototype. Two of our client-hottest ideas score poorly with judges for exactly that reason — they demo like products that already exist. Both views appear below; today we pick with the judges' view.

**How the scores work (footnote for every table):** an independent judging pass scored all 64 ideas *blind* against the official weights, deliberately ignoring our internal shortlist order; it published numeric scores (out of 100) for its top 8 — other rows show "—". Separately, a fresh web check on 2026-07-15 tested whether anyone already ships each top idea. The **Verdict** word (Strong / Good / Risky / Out) is our translation of the judge score plus that web check.

### Group 1 in detail

#### 1 · Reason-Code Honesty (C28)

- **What it is.** An auditor for decision letters. It doesn't judge whether a loan denial was *right* — it judges whether the *stated reason* was *true*, by re-running the decision with that one factor fixed. It also spots patterns: which reasons are decorative across the whole book, and whether false reasons cluster in particular customer groups (a fair-lending red flag).
- **What the demo looks like.** We show a pretend lender we built, sending real-looking denial letters. The tool works through a batch, and on screen three letters turn red: "stated reason: income — but income wasn't the deciding factor." We open one, show the re-run side by side, and then show the gate refusing to send the next untrue letter. Close with the accuracy scorecard: we planted 40 lies in 500 letters; it found 38, falsely accused 1.
- **The five questions.** Pay? — yes, compliance budget, with a dated CFPB circular (2026-05-05) forcing the issue. Real engineering? — yes: controlled re-execution of decisions, not text generation. Six weeks? — yes, smallest build in the top group. Already built? — no (checked 2026-07-15; Zest, FairPlay, SolasAI, Stratyfy all do adjacent things, none do this). Demos well? — yes if staged carefully; the risk is subtlety, so the demo script matters more than usual.
- **What could kill it.** A judge who thinks "isn't this just explainable-AI tooling?" — the answer (existing tools explain decisions; this one catches letters that lie about them) must be rehearsed. Nothing found in the market kills it.
- **If we pick it, week 1 looks like:** build the pretend loan-decision engine and the letter generator, and plant the first set of false reasons — that gives us our answer key and the demo backbone in one step.

#### 2 · Postcode Shockwave (C63)

- **What it is.** An early-warning system that treats a local economic shock the way public health treats an outbreak. Employer-level watch on salary deposits → shock declared → affected customers identified, including second-order businesses → help offered before the first missed payment.
- **What the demo looks like.** A map of a model town. We announce "the plant closed" and watch the tool work: 340 workers found, then — the moment that lands — the café, the landlord, and the car-loan customer two streets over light up too. Then the drafted outreach plan and the frontline briefing.
- **The five questions.** Pay? — the honest weakness: collections-prevention and community-lending budgets both benefit, neither clearly owns it. Real engineering? — yes: second-order effects require reasoning over a web of connections, not a threshold alert. Six weeks? — yes, the model town is a contained build. Already built? — no (confirmed 2026-07-15): payroll data is aggregated for economic reports, never for pre-emptive help. Demos well? — the best in all 64 ideas.
- **What could kill it.** The "who buys this?" question in Q&A — we prep the answer, we don't improvise it.
- **If we pick it, week 1 looks like:** design the model town (employers, workers, shops, dependencies) with the plant closure planted — the test data *is* the product's proving ground.

#### 3 · Accord (C47)

- **What it is.** A collections offer engine. Reads real transactions instead of interviewing the borrower, simulates every allowed relief permutation against affordability *and* the bank's lifetime loss, hands the rep one pre-approved offer. The full product also negotiates within policy and tends plans daily — those parts don't fit the competition window and would be shown as the roadmap.
- **What the demo looks like.** A collections call, side by side: the old way (20-minute interview, plan breaks in month 3) vs. the new way (offer ready before the call connects, and it holds). Headline number: cure rate / re-default rate on our test borrowers.
- **The five questions.** Pay? — the strongest answer in the pool: this is our live Barclays engagement's own success metric. Real engineering? — moderate: simulation over policy is solid but familiar. Six weeks? — only the offer engine; we must resist scope creep. Already built? — partly: Salient (AI hardship calls, $25M revenue) and C&R Software + AperiData (2026, real-time affordability-driven plan changes) are named comparisons judges may know. Demos well? — good, not magical.
- **What could kill it.** The originality question. If a judge says "Salient does this," our answer is the simulate-everything + tend-the-plan loop — which is exactly the part we can't build in 6 weeks. That's the trap.
- **If we pick it, week 1 looks like:** build the test borrower population (transactions with planted affordability truths) and encode one bank's relief policy as rules.

#### 4 · Agent-Purchase Referee (C06)

- **What it is.** The first dispute desk for purchases made by AI assistants. Reconstructs authorization vs. action, classifies (customer-approved / assistant error / fraud), files in network format — and writes the missing procedure manual and precedent log as a by-product, which may be the more valuable artifact.
- **What the demo looks like.** Three disputes come in: one the customer really did authorize (and forgot), one where the assistant misread an instruction, one fraud. The tool untangles each from the authorization records, decides, files — and page one of the growing procedure manual appears on screen.
- **The five questions.** Pay? — the honest weakness: volume at the last published count was hundreds of disputes (Dec 2025, before the new rails launched), so impact is a forecast. Real engineering? — yes: reconstructing "what was the assistant told vs. what did it do" from records is genuinely new work. Six weeks? — yes; the data must be invented either way, which suits the rules. Already built? — no, verified across all four card-network rails. Demos well? — very; it's the only idea that makes judges feel the future arriving.
- **What could kill it.** An impact-focused judge doing the volume math out loud. The counter — "whoever writes the procedure owns the category" — is a bet, and we'd be asking the judges to take it with us.
- **If we pick it, week 1 looks like:** design the dispute case format and generate the first 50 test disputes with planted ground truth (which classification is correct and why).

### Group 2 in detail

#### 5 · Last-Door Case Agent (C64)

- **What it is / demo.** The "prove there was no other way" gate before repossession. Demo: a case file opens, the tool enumerates 23 alternatives, models each, finds one the humans missed — a government scheme — and the repossession stops. In a second case, everything truly fails, and the certificate prints.
- **Watch-outs.** Building the answer key ("what *was* the best alternative?") is genuinely hard — this is the top-8 idea most likely to eat weeks 3–4. Also unclear which budget (compliance vs. recoveries) buys the certificate.
- **Week 1:** write 10 test cases *by hand* with domain care, before generating any at scale — if we can't define "best alternative" on paper, the build isn't ready.

#### 6 · Main-Street Early Warning (C62)

- **What it is / demo.** Seasonal-dip vs. real-decline judgment over small-business accounts, plus the briefing and options pack. Demo: two businesses with near-identical dips; the tool clears one, flags the other, and shows its reasoning — then the drafted call brief.
- **Watch-outs.** If our judgment component only marginally beats a simple alert threshold, the depth story collapses. The discriminating test *is* the entry.
- **Week 1:** build the seasonal-decoy test set first and baseline it against a dumb threshold — we need to know week 1 whether there's daylight.

#### 7 · Zombie Agent Hunter (C15)

- **What it is / demo.** Orphaned-AI-credential detection by cross-referencing systems that were never designed to agree. Demo ends on the number: "six can move money; no living owner."
- **Watch-outs.** "Why not Astrix or Oasis?" needs a rehearsed answer (payment-authority depth). Depth ceiling is real: the reasoning is joins plus lifecycle logic.

#### 9 · Ear on Every Call (C61)

#### 10 · Evidence Chaser (C23)

#### In brief

**Break Detective (C05).** It investigated the aged reconciliation breaks the matching software gives up on, tracing history and proposing resolving entries overnight. It was shortlist #3 and strong on every question except one — until the 2026-07-15 web check found SmartStream "Smart Agents" (publicly available 2026-03-26, announced 2025-12-04) and Duco (2026-05-27) already ship exactly this. Beaten to market; the finding also taints the one-sided framings of C20 and C17.

**Fail Fixer Pair (C17).** It had both banks' agents investigate a failed trade and negotiate to shared facts instead of four days of email. The two-sided version needs a consenting bank on the other side to agree to play; without one it collapses into C05's territory, which is itself now beaten to market. Dropped for needing a counterparty that has to opt in.

**Wind-Down Clerk (C33).** It ran a small business's complete shutdown — every account, license, filing, and final payroll — end-to-end. The fatal flaw: the value goes to a customer who is leaving the bank, so the bank that would pay for it gets nothing back. Dropped unless reframed around retention or estate relationships.

**Nightly Money Router (C41).** It swept small-business cash nightly to its best use. Ramp, Brex, and Mercury already ship small-business treasury sweeping, so the space is taken. Dropped; the only piece worth keeping is a version built into the bank, priced against the bank's own credit-line rates.

**Fee Forensics / RateWatch (C43).** It audited business fees and flagged overcharges. Contingency fee-auditing is an established, staffed industry, so the core is a solved business. Dropped; only the autonomous renegotiation/switch-execution part (which the industry doesn't do) is worth revisiting.

**Certified Payroll Ghostwriter (C45).** It handled certified-payroll paperwork so small contractors could survive bidding government work. ADP would build or acquire this, and LCPtracker already serves the segment. Dropped — no defensible position against the payroll incumbents.

**Bindable (C51).** It underwrote and bound insurance coverage for AI-agent operators. AIUC already ships it — the Beazley paper plus ElevenLabs' $50M policy, 2026-05-15 — and two independent research passes reached the same park call against the same competitor. Dropped; only small-business-tier operators below AIUC's enterprise motion remain.

**Big-Decision Second Chair (C59).** It sat on a live call while a customer was being scammed into cashing out a pension, showing the true cost mid-call and offering a 48-hour cool-off. The live-call version can't respond fast enough — the physics of latency — and a replay-after-the-fact version isn't the product. Dropped unless re-scoped to friction between contacts.

**Fiver-a-Month Adviser (C60).** It offered always-on personal financial guidance for a few pounds a month to the unadvised. Cleo, Plum, and Chip already occupy that positioning. Dropped; only a bank-built version that can actually make the changes within agreed limits — which the consumer apps lack — remains.
