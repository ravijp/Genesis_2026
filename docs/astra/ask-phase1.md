# Phase 1 — the idea

You have a factual brief on a system that reads customer conversations and remembers them. It tells you
what exists, what has been measured, what the market looks like, and who else is in the space.

**I am not asking you to review it. I am asking you for the idea.**

---

## The one thing that is fixed

There is a working capability, and it is the raw material you are building with:

> A system that **reads every customer conversation** an institution has, **extracts what the customer
> said with the exact quote attached**, **remembers it against that customer across months and
> channels**, and **re-values old evidence when new evidence arrives** — so something said in January
> that meant nothing at the time can become important in April. A human sees a decision-ready case at
> the end; the system never contacts anybody.

That capability is real, it is built, and it is what you have to work with.

**Everything else is yours to decide.** What problem it solves. Who buys it. What it predicts. What
industry. What it is called. What appears on a screen. How it is sold. The brief describes one team's
current answers — treat them as one possibility among many, not a starting point you owe anything to.

**I am not asking what is safe to pitch given what exists today. Do not optimise for what is already
done. Optimise for what should exist. If the right answer throws the current framing away entirely,
throw it away and tell me what to build instead.**

---

## What I want

**The best idea that can be built from that capability, a clear account of how a bank would actually
run it, and a demo that makes a room want it.**

Think of this as the version you would build if it were yours and you had a year and a real budget.

**Do not constrain yourself to what is currently built or currently measured.** Re-pointing the system
at a different problem costs about a day of content authoring and under a dollar of model spend. I can
build. If the idea is strong enough I will build it properly. **What I cannot do is make a mediocre
idea interesting**, so give me the ambitious one and let me worry about the work.

The only hard constraint: it must be **demonstrable on synthetic data**. I cannot show real customer
conversations.

---

## 1. What is the problem worth solving here?

Something can read every conversation, remember every customer, and change its mind about the past.
**What is that actually for?**

The brief states one answer. I want yours, arrived at independently. Where is the pain sharp enough
that someone pays, urgent enough that they act this year, and genuinely unaddressed by what they
already own?

**Interrogate the premise.** The brief frames the problem as *banks read every conversation and
remember no customer* — and that framing drove every downstream choice. Is it the real problem? Is it
the most *valuable* problem near this capability? Is it the most *sellable* one? Those three can have
different answers, and the gap between them is where products die. If the framing is subtly wrong —
true but not urgent, real but not ownable — say exactly where it breaks and give me the better one.

## 2. What is the product?

The dream version. Concrete:

- **What it does** for the institution that buys it.
- **Who signs** for it, and which budget it comes out of.
- **What changes on the day it goes live** — what someone does differently that they could not do
  before.

Ambition is welcome. Vagueness is not. "A conversation intelligence platform" is not a product; it is a
brochure.

## 3. The sentence

The sentence that makes an executive lean forward. Two if you must, but one is better.

Said out loud in a room, it should make someone who buys software think *I want that, and I know where
it goes in my business.* Write it. If you cannot get it that short, the idea is not finished — keep
working until you can.

## 4. What is this, in the buyer's world?

Products get bought inside a category, and the category decides who the competitor is and which budget
pays. *Conversation analytics* already exists, is crowded, and has entrenched incumbents.

What category does this belong in — one that already exists and can be won, or a new one worth naming?
If new, name it in words a buyer would use without needing it explained.

## 5. How does a bank actually run this? — and how is it sold?

**This is the half I understand least and need most.** An idea that cannot be described as a thing a
bank installs, staffs and pays for is not yet a product.

### Inside one bank

- **Where does it sit?** What does it read from, what does it write to, what does it touch. Be
  specific about the systems — contact-centre platform, CRM, case management, feature store, data
  warehouse, whatever is actually right.
- **What has to be true on their side** before it works at all, and what is the realistic state of that
  in a bank that is not a technology leader.
- **Who operates it day to day**, and whose job changes.
- **The adoption path.** What is the first deployment, what does it become, and how long does that
  realistically take.
- **What is genuinely hard about deploying this into a bank** as opposed to any other enterprise.

### Across many banks

- **Is this one product configured differently, or several products?** A top-five card issuer, a
  regional bank and a credit union have different volumes, different problems and different
  sophistication. Do they buy the same thing?
- **Can the same installation serve different uses at different banks** — retention at one, complaints
  or collections or fraud at another — off the same underlying capability? If so, is that a **strength
  to sell or a trap that makes every sale bespoke?** The original framing of this system was a
  horizontal layer serving several teams at once; the current pitch narrowed to one use. Tell me which
  is right and why.
- **The land-and-expand motion.** What is the wedge — the smallest thing a bank buys first — and what
  does it grow into? What does the second year look like if the first goes well?
- **Platform or point solution**, and what that choice costs in the first sale.

## 6. The demo — the centre of what I am asking

Seven minutes, live, in front of two finance executives.

**Design it.** Not principles for a demo — the demo:

- **The story.** Who is the customer in it, what happens to them, over what span of time.
- **Beat by beat.** What happens first, second, third. What is on screen at each moment.
- **The moment.** The single beat the whole thing is built around — where someone stops watching
  software and starts thinking about their own business. Tell me what it is and why it lands.
- **The ending.** What the last thirty seconds are, and the sentence I say as I stop.

Assume I can build whatever you describe. If it needs a screen or data that does not exist yet, design
it anyway and mark what has to be made.

Then tell me **what the demo must not do** — the beat that feels impressive to whoever built it and
lands flat, or badly, with someone who runs a business.

## 7. Why does this win?

Two things, briefly:

- **What is genuinely new** once the marketing is stripped off — and is it new in a way anyone pays
  for? Novelty nobody values is worth nothing, and so is value copied in a quarter.
- **Why it beats the obvious objection** — that the bank already owns tools which read calls, and
  already runs models that predict the thing you are predicting.

## 8. What do I build first?

Short and ranked. What has to exist for the demo in §6 to be real, in what order, roughly what each
takes.

---

## How to answer

- **Lead with the idea.** First paragraph, no preamble, no restating the brief.
- **Be concrete.** Words I can say, screens I can show, systems it plugs into, things I can build.
- **Commit.** One idea, argued properly. If you genuinely cannot separate two, give the second in a
  short paragraph at the end and say which you would build.
- **Do not hedge and do not soften.** If the honest answer is that the interesting product here is
  something quite different from what the brief describes, that is exactly what I want to hear.
