# Phase 2 — assess the team's own analysis

You have now given me your independent read. **Hold on to it.**

Below are the working documents the team produced before you saw any of this. They contain their own
analysis, their own criticism of the idea, the choices they made and the reasoning behind each, several
claims they corrected or withdrew, and a great deal of tactical advice about what to say and what not
to say in the room.

I withheld all of it from you deliberately, so that your phase 1 answer would be genuinely yours.

## What I am asking

**Judge this work. Do not adopt it.**

I am not showing you these documents so you can align with them. I am showing you so you can tell me
where the team's reasoning holds, where it fails, and what it never considered. Their conclusions carry
no authority with you. Several are probably wrong. Some may be badly wrong in ways that are hard to see
from inside the project, which is the whole reason you are reading this cold.

**1. Where were you wrong?** Anywhere their analysis changed your mind, say so explicitly: what you
concluded in phase 1, what in their reasoning moved you, and what you now think. Intellectual honesty
here is worth more to me than consistency.

**2. Where are they wrong?** Anywhere you disagree and are holding your position, say so and argue it.
Be specific about which claim, which document, which line of reasoning. **Do not defer to them because
they have more context than you.** More context is also how people talk themselves into things.

**3. What did they miss entirely?** Things absent from both your phase 1 answer and their documents
count double here — but so does anything you spotted that they did not, and anything they clearly
looked at and then dropped for a bad reason.

**4. The evidence, read hostilely.** I did not ask you about this in the last round because I wanted
the idea first. Now read the measurements in the brief the way a sceptical technical reviewer would —
including the ones the team lost: a chance-gate failure at `p=0.076`, an ablation where switching every
scoring mechanism off *beat* the full system on the headline stratum, a whole-portfolio ranking of 8th
of 9 arms, records that reversed across two corpus rebuilds, and a reader that finds churn intent in
1 of 8 real credit-card documents. **Which results actually support the central claim, which are
neutral, and which argue against it?** Name anything being over-read. Name anything measured and
under-used. And tell me whether the evidence base supports the idea you proposed, the idea they are
pitching, both, or neither.

**5. Grade the self-criticism itself.** These documents are unusually self-critical: they publish
losses, withdraw claims, and record what not to say. **Is that rigour, or is it a substitute for
rigour?** A team can be scrupulously honest about a hole and still have a hole. Tell me which is
happening here, and whether the self-criticism is aimed at the things that actually matter or at the
things that are easiest to be honest about.

**6. The choices they made.** They chose one direction over twelve others, one market, one buyer, one
demo, one value chain. For each significant choice, is it right? Where a choice is defensible but not
optimal, say which. Where a choice looks like it was made for the wrong reason — sunk cost, what was
already built, what was easiest to demo — name it.

## How to answer

- **Your phase 1 answer is the baseline.** Every section should be legible as agreement, update, or
  disagreement against what you already said.
- **Be specific.** "They underweight X" is worth nothing without which claim and why.
- The tactical advice in these documents — what to say, what to cut, what never to mention — is itself
  a set of judgments you can assess. Some of it may be defending against the wrong risks.
- Do not be diplomatic about the quality of reasoning. If a piece of it is motivated, circular, or
  built on a number that cannot bear the weight put on it, say exactly that.
