# Defending the score — reproducibility, model change, and the confidence float

**2026-09-09, for the 09-11 gate.** The question this answers has not been asked yet in any red-team
pass, and it is the most dangerous one a numerate executive has available:

> *"Your score depends on an LLM reading a transcript. LLM outputs are not reproducible and the
> confidence values are not real probabilities. What happens when that model is retired, or when a
> better one comes out and you switch? Does my whole feature change under me? Do I have to
> re-validate everything? How is this a controlled input to a regulated model?"*

---

## The four sentences you actually say

Say these, in this order, and stop. Everything below is for the follow-up.

> **"Half of that score is deterministic and half of it isn't, and I'll tell you which half.**
> **The arithmetic is fixed code — same signals in, bit-identical score out, and there's a unit test
> that asserts exactly that. The reading is a model, and a model is not bit-stable, so we don't
> promise determinism there — we promise a fingerprint: every score carries the model id and the
> prompt hash that produced it, and re-running that fingerprint reproduces the score.**
> **You're asking what happens when the model changes. We've already changed it and measured it —
> Nova Lite against Claude Haiku on the same 282 conversations, and I can show you the delta.**
> **So model change here is a re-baselining run against a frozen benchmark, not a re-validation of
> your propensity model."**

**If he pushes on the confidence number specifically** — and the good ones will — do not defend it:

> **"That's the weakest thing in the design and you've found it. The model emits a confidence float
> and the literature is clear that verbalised LLM confidence is overconfident and coarse. We have an
> ablation that turns it off. Before this goes anywhere near your model, that float gets bucketed or
> calibrated against a held-out set — it does not go into a regulated feature as a raw model output."**

**Conceding this costs nothing and buys the room.** Defending it is how you lose the one person
present who has read a calibration paper.

## 1. What is and is not reproducible — the honest split

Do not say "our score is deterministic" unqualified. It is a half-truth and the half that is false is
the half a modeller will test.

| Layer | Reproducible? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Score arithmetic** — accumulation, decay, corroboration, cross-channel, saturation | **Yes, bit-identical** | `memory.py:91-114`. `tests/test_memory.py:256-263` (`test_scoring_is_deterministic`) runs identical input through three fresh ledgers and asserts identical floats |
| **Signal type** the reader assigns | **No** — model- and version-dependent | Measured: Nova Lite vs Haiku disagree on 4/282 conversations of coverage and on 5/80 crossings |
| **Verbatim quote** | **No, but it is guarded** | Exact-substring checked; unverifiable quotes are discarded. Measured: Haiku 0 non-verbatim, Nova Lite 10 non-verbatim + 9 relocated, on the same 282 |
| **The `confidence` float** | **No, and it is uncalibrated** | See §3. This is the weak link |
| **A score, given a pinned fingerprint** | **Yes, in practice** | Cache key covers model id + `prompt_sha` + messages + tools. See §4 for where it leaks |

**And one piece of evidence better than the unit test, generated by accident on 2026-09-09.** The
published 30-seed sweep was re-run from a different commit (`d024540-dirty` → `79fad49`) a month after
the original. The output file was **byte-identical except for the recorded git SHA and the elapsed
seconds** — every arm, every comparison, every p-value unchanged. **That is a reproduction, not a
test**, and it is the kind of thing worth having done before someone asks whether the numbers move.

**The formula, so you can say it if asked:**
`score = 1 − exp(−saturation × Σ(confidence × decay) × multiplier)`, where `multiplier` compounds the
corroboration, cross-channel and escalation bonuses (`memory.py:97-114`).

**The line to hold:** *the arithmetic is reproducible, the reading is auditable, and the score is
reproducible given a fingerprint.* Three different claims, three different strengths. Collapsing them
into "it's deterministic" is the overclaim that gets you caught.

## 2. The governance framing — and a correction that matters more than the framing

### SR 11-7 is dead. Do not cite it on 09-11.

**The current pitch cites SR 11-7 twice** (`01-THE-STORY.md` objection playbook, `03-REFERENCE.md` §3).
It was **superseded on 2026-04-17**.

- **SR 26-2, "Revised Guidance on Model Risk Management," issued 2026-04-17**, supersedes SR 11-7
  (2011-04-04) and SR 21-8 (2021-04-09).
  *(Federal Reserve, https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/SR2602.htm, retrieved 2026-09-09.)*
- It is **one joint interagency document** issued by the OCC, the Federal Reserve and the FDIC
  together — not a Fed letter later paralleled. **OCC Bulletin 2026-13** (2026-04-17) rescinds
  **OCC Bulletin 2011-12**. National banks are not under the old regime either.
- **Applicability: banking organizations with over $30 billion in total assets.** Check the
  counterparty against that threshold before leaning on it.

**Citing rescinded guidance to a bank audience is the single fastest way to be marked as not having
checked** — the same failure the CFPB-enforcement argument was cut for. Knowing the guidance changed
five months ago is worth more in that room than any answer in this document.

### And the new guidance helps, in a specific and limited way

Verbatim, SR 26-2 attachment p.3:

> *"the term 'model' refers to a complex quantitative method, system, or approach that applies
> statistical, economic, or financial theories to process input data into quantitative estimates. The
> term 'model' in this guidance excludes simple arithmetic calculations, such as those found within
> spreadsheets, as well as deterministic rule-based processes and software where there are no
> statistical, economic, or financial theories underpinning their design or use."*

**The ledger arithmetic is a deterministic rule-based process.** That is a strong reading — but do not
push it to "our score is not a model." The half-lives are argued from real-world signal persistence,
which a validator could fairly call a theory of the world. **The defensible claim is that the
arithmetic sits at the light end of the tiering, not that it escapes the perimeter.**

And on generative AI, footnote 3, p.3:

> *"Generative AI and agentic AI models are novel and rapidly evolving. As such, they are not within
> the scope of this guidance... the principles described in this guidance apply to traditional
> statistical and quantitative models and non-generative, non-agentic AI models."*

**Read this carefully before you use it, because it cuts both ways.** It is a scope carve-out, not a
definitional exclusion, and it does not say GenAI carries no model-risk obligation — it says *this
document's machinery does not cover it*. To a COO that is not relief; it means **there is no settled
supervisory playbook for the extractor**, so the bank's own AI governance policy governs and we have
to supply what that policy asks for. Say it that way round. Offering "the regulator doesn't cover it"
as good news to a risk-literate executive reads as either naive or evasive.

### The three-tier framing — use it, but do not dress it up as industry vocabulary

| Tier | Component | What it is | Change-control regime |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | The extractor (LLM) | **Feature generator.** Emits typed signals with verbatim quotes | Pinned version + fingerprint + re-baselining on change (§5) |
| **2** | The ledger | **Deterministic transformation.** Accumulate, decay, threshold | Unit-tested; changes are code changes under normal SDLC |
| **3** | Their propensity model | **The decision model.** Consumes our score as one feature | Their existing validation and champion/challenger cycle, untouched |

**Verified: this vocabulary is ours, not the industry's.** It does not appear in SR 26-2, and 2026
vendor commentary on the GenAI carve-out does not use it — Databricks argues the opposite, for one
shared registry with no tiering. **So present it as how *we* propose to structure the engagement, not
as how the regime already works.** Presenting an invented framework as standard practice to a model
risk function is a one-question death.

**Where it holds:** SR 26-2's vendor section is squarely about tier 1, and it is satisfiable —
*"Sound practice includes developing an understanding of the vendor model, including its conceptual
soundness, design, development data, and performance"* (pp.11-12, §VII). We can supply all four.

**Where it breaks:** the tiering does not reduce the total burden, it relocates it. SR 26-2 requires
the consuming model's owner to understand, monitor and outcome-test what it ingests. **Tier 3 inherits
tier 1's instability.** A validator who says *"I don't care what you call your extractor, my model's
feature moved"* is correct, and the answer to that is §5, not the diagram.

## 3. The confidence float — the recommendation, and it is a build item

### What the code actually does, and it is worse than the docs imply

`memory.py:99`:

```python
weight = s.confidence if cfg.confidence_weighting else 1.0
```

**The confidence value is a direct multiplier on every signal's contribution to the score**, and
`confidence_weighting` defaults to `True` (`config.py:73`). It is not decoration and it is not a
tiebreak. Three consequences, all of which a good modeller will find:

1. **The model's self-reported confidence propagates linearly into the score.**
2. **The offline lexicon's confidence is a hardcoded per-cue constant** (`corpus_lexicon.py`,
   `extract_lexicon.py`) and flows through the *identical field* into the *identical arithmetic*. A
   regex's 0.92 and a model's 0.92 are treated as the same quantity. They are not the same quantity.
   **Nothing in the code distinguishes them by source and nothing calibrates either.**
3. *(Do not conflate this with the investigator agent's own `confidence` field at
   `agent/schemas.py:42` — that one is display-only and never touches scoring.)*

### What the literature says — say it before they do

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Xiong et al., "Can LLMs Express Their Uncertainty?", ICLR 2024, arXiv:2306.13063 (2023-06-22)** | Across five LLMs and five dataset types, models are **consistently overconfident** when verbalising confidence, "potentially imitating human patterns of expressing confidence." The paper most directly on point |
| **Tian et al., "Just Ask for Calibration", EMNLP 2023, arXiv:2305.14975 (2023-05-24)** | **RLHF degrades calibration** relative to the pretrained base. But — and this is the case *for* the float — verbalised confidence beats the model's own token probabilities, "often reducing expected calibration error by a relative 50%" on TriviaQA, SciQ and TruthfulQA |
| **OpenAI, GPT-4 Technical Report, arXiv:2303.08774 (2023-03)** | Verbatim: *"the pre-trained model is highly calibrated... However, after the post-training process, the calibration is reduced."* Their own flagship, their own disclosure. **Cite the qualitative finding only — the numbers are in a plot, not a table** |
| **Kadavath et al., arXiv:2207.05221 (2022-07-11)** | Larger models self-evaluate "P(True)" reasonably well **on multiple-choice formats**. That is not the free-form per-signal float we emit — do not cite this as support |
| **Kalai et al., "Calibrated Language Models Must Hallucinate", arXiv:2311.14648 (2023-11)** | A calibrated LM is mathematically forced to hallucinate on rare facts. "Just calibrate it better" has a **hard floor**, not merely a practical one |

### How much does the float actually matter? Measured, not argued

`arms.py:310` already defines a `no-confidence-weighting` ablation, so this is measurable rather than
arguable. **Measured 2026-09-09 across 30 independent datasets** — `earshot run --seed 1..30
--customers 1500`, offline lexicon reader, $0 — comparing each ablation's flagged set against the
`full-ledger` arm at the top-150 of 1,500 customers (the 10% review-capacity budget):

| Mechanism removed | Mean top-150 overlap | Min | Max |
|---|---|---|---|
| escalation | **100.0%** | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| cross-channel | 92.7% | 88.7% | 97.3% |
| decay | 92.4% | 88.7% | 94.7% |
| **confidence weighting** | **80.0%** | 74.7% | 86.7% |
| corroboration | 79.8% | 69.3% | 88.7% |

**Read this precisely, because the honest version is narrower than the tempting one.** Confidence
weighting and corroboration are **jointly the most load-bearing mechanisms** — 80.0% and 79.8%,
statistically indistinguishable at 30 datasets. Confidence is not *uniquely* the biggest lever. But
the point stands and it is uncomfortable: **turning the confidence float off changes about one in five
of the customers your retention team would work**, and it is the one input in the design with no
calibration and a literature against it.

*(A single stale 400-customer run suggested confidence was uniquely dominant. On the current corpus at
30 datasets it is not. The narrower claim is the one to make.)*

**One incidental finding, outside this document's brief but worth someone's attention:** the
escalation bonus changes **nothing at all** — 100.0% overlap on every one of 30 datasets. At this
operating point it is inert. That does not refute AT-52's "keep all four mechanisms", which was
decided on different evidence, but **anyone about to defend the escalation term on stage should know
it currently moves no ranking decision.**

### Recommendation: bucket it. Do not keep the raw float, do not go straight to calibration.

| Option | What it buys | What it costs | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Keep the raw float** | Nothing defensible | An uncalibrated model output linearly weighting a regulated feature | **No** |
| **Binary presence/absence** (`confidence_weighting=False`) | Maximum defensibility; already implemented | Throws away real signal — Tian et al. show the float does carry information | Too blunt |
| **Bucket to 3 tiers** (weak / moderate / strong) | Keeps ordinal information, discards false precision. Robust to the coarse-saturation problem — a model that only ever says 0.9 or 1.0 lands in one bucket either way | ~1 day: a mapping function, a config flag, and re-running the ablation | **★ Do this** |
| **Post-hoc calibration** against a held-out answer key | The real fix. Store the *calibration map*, not the raw number. **And it is the answer to "do I re-validate everything": no — you re-run a cheap calibration step, not a full model validation** | Needs a labelled held-out set per model. Must be re-fitted on every model swap | **Do this second**, once a client's data exists |
| **Self-consistency over N samples** | Better uncertainty estimates | N× inference cost — at $1.58/1,000 that is affordable, but it is a real change | Not now |
| **Two-model agreement** | Most robust | 2× cost. And our own Nova-vs-Haiku data shows it surfaces genuine disagreement rather than free consensus | Not now |

**Build cost of the recommendation: about a day**, and the ablation harness to prove it already
exists. **This is a build item, not a talking point.** It will not be done by 09-11 — say it as the
next thing you build, which is a stronger answer than a defence.

## 4. Reproducibility by fingerprint — and exactly where it leaks

**The claim:** every score carries the model id and prompt hash that produced it, and re-running that
fingerprint reproduces the score.

**Verified as far as it goes:** the cache key covers model id, `prompt_sha`, messages and tools. A
model-id change correctly busts the cache rather than silently returning old-model results.

**Three leaks. Know them before an auditor finds them:**

1. **Sampling parameters are not in the cache key.** Temperature and `max_tokens` are not covered. A
   temperature change would silently reuse cached results. **This is a real defect, it is cheap to
   fix, and you should not claim full fingerprint coverage until it is.**
2. **`pipeline_sha` covers five modules — corpus, lexicon and ledger — but not `extract_model.py` or
   `llm/bedrock.py`.** Reader-side code changes are invisible to the fingerprint.
3. **Persisted ledger entries carry `cue_id` (prompt version) but never the model id.** Model identity
   lives only in run manifests — and **the deployed Lambda path writes no manifest at all**. So for a
   score produced by the deployed pipeline, the model cannot currently be recovered from the stored
   record.

**Therefore the honest answer to "can you trace a score to a model and prompt" is *partially*, and in
the research harness rather than the deployed path.** Say "the fingerprint is designed in and three
gaps are open, here they are" — a named gap with a fix is a normal engineering conversation; a claim
that collapses under one question is not.

**Where fingerprinting fails as an audit answer even when complete:** it proves *what* produced a
score, not that the score was *right*. An auditor asking "is this feature fit for purpose" is not
answered by a hash. That question is answered by the frozen benchmark, §5.

## 5. The model-migration protocol — the strongest answer available

**Frame model change as routine, planned and documented. It is the only framing that survives.** A
vendor who implies their model will never change is not credible to anyone who has run production
software.

**And this is not a promise — it is something we have already done.** Every figure below is verified
against the machine-written results JSON `artifacts/runs/reader-coverage-20260809-1ee962fd608f...`,
not typed into a document:

| | Amazon Nova Lite | Claude Haiku 4.5 |
|---|---|---|
| coverage, same 282 conversations | 181 / 282 | 177 / 282 |
| crossings | 60 / 80 | 65 / 80 |
| cost per 1,000 conversations | $0.0982 | $1.58 |
| p50 latency | 873 ms | 1,333 ms |
| quotes not verbatim | 10 | 0 |
| quotes relocated | 9 | 0 |

**Same input set, same seed, same config hash, same threshold — verified from the manifests, not
asserted.** Total spend for the whole comparison: **$0.027705**.

> **"You're asking what happens when the model changes. We've already changed it and measured it, on
> the same 282 conversations, for under three cents."**

That sentence is the single strongest thing in this document. It converts an unanswerable
hypothetical into a demonstrated capability.

### The written protocol

When the reader model changes, in order:

1. **Freeze the benchmark.** Run old and new readers over the same conversation set with the same
   prompt version. The harness is `tools/reader_coverage.py` — **a parameterised, reusable tool
   (`--reader`, `--model`), not a one-off script.**
2. **Measure signal-level agreement** — per-family coverage, and quote fidelity (verbatim and
   relocation counts), which is where Nova Lite actually lost.
3. **Measure score-level rank stability** — top-K overlap and rank correlation on the same customers.
   **Not value stability.**
4. **Re-fit the calibration map** on the held-out set (once §3's second recommendation is built).
5. **Report the delta** to the client's model risk function as a written change record.
6. **Champion/challenger on their side.** The new reader's feature runs as challenger against the
   incumbent feature before it replaces it. This is a cycle their validation team already runs.

**The cost claim, and keep it modest:** steps 1-3 cost cents at our volumes. **Steps 5 and 6 cost
their team time, and we do not control that.** Do not tell a COO that model migration is free.

### Rank stability, not value stability — rigour or dodge?

**Interrogated, and it holds — but only because of what consumes the score.** The consuming use is
top-decile ranking into a fixed-capacity queue, and path 1 feeds a feature into a model that re-fits
on its own data. Neither consumes an absolute value. **The metric should match the use, and for this
use rank stability is the correct metric, not a lenient one.**

**Where it becomes a dodge, and be honest about this:** the moment anyone proposes an eligibility
*threshold* on the raw score, absolute stability starts to matter and rank overlap stops being
sufficient. Path 3 (eligibility flag) is exactly that case. **So: rank stability is the right bar for
paths 1 and 2, and it is not sufficient for path 3.** Saying which is which is what makes it rigour.

## 6. Where we must not overclaim

Model risk regimes require auditability and repeatability **of process**, not bit-identical outputs.
Claiming more than is true is worse than explaining the regime honestly. The line, precisely:

| Say this | Never say this |
|---|---|
| "The score arithmetic is deterministic and there is a test that asserts it" | "The system is deterministic" |
| "Every score carries a model and prompt fingerprint — and three coverage gaps are open, here they are" | "Every score is fully reproducible" |
| "The confidence float is a raw model output, it is uncalibrated, and bucketing it is the next thing we build" | "Confidence is calibrated" / silence on the question |
| "We've already swapped the model once and measured the delta" | "Model changes won't affect your feature" |
| "The arithmetic sits at the light end of SR 26-2's tiering" | "Our score is not a model under SR 26-2" |
| "GenAI is outside SR 26-2's scope, so your own AI governance policy governs and we'll supply what it needs" | "The regulator doesn't cover this, so there's nothing to do" |

## Could not verify

- **The GPT-4 Figure 8 calibration numbers.** The finding is qualitative in the text; the numbers are
  in a plot. Cite the sentence, never a number.
- **Two 2026-numbered arXiv preprints** on confidence saturation and a confidence benchmark surfaced
  during research have internally inconsistent dating and are **deliberately excluded** from §3. The
  four citations used are all verifiable.
- **Whether the counterparty is above SR 26-2's $30 billion threshold.** Unknowable until the client
  is named; it determines whether the guidance binds them at all.
- **FDIC's own FIL number** for the joint 2026 guidance — not chased. The Fed and OCC instruments are
  confirmed.
- **The "agrees to the last digit" claim** (`0.6526618648909545` vs `0.652662`). No test asserts it;
  `feed.py:verify()` never compares scores in strict mode. It is a human comparing a full float
  against a `.6f`-rounded print. **True as far as it goes, but it is an eyeball, not a check** — do
  not describe it as verified.
- **Whether the confidence ablation behaves the same with a *model* reader.** The 30-dataset ablation
  used the offline lexicon, whose confidences are hardcoded constants. **The measurement that matters
  most — how much a *model-emitted* confidence moves the ranking — has not been run**, and it is the
  one this whole section is about. It would cost real money; it is the right next spend.
- **Whether bucketing preserves the ranking.** Recommended in §3 on reasoning, not measured. The
  harness exists; run it before promising a client the change is neutral.
